## What it is

VO2 max intervals are repetitions of three to five minutes at near-maximum
aerobic effort, with recovery jogs of roughly equal length between them.

VO2 max is the ceiling on how much oxygen your body can take in, deliver, and
use per minute. It's the size of your aerobic engine. These sessions are the
most direct way to make that engine bigger, and they're deliberately
uncomfortable.

## How this differs from ordinary intervals

"[Interval training](/blog/interval-run)" is a broad category — any hard rep
with recovery between counts. VO2 max intervals are a specific dose inside that
category, and the specificity is the whole point.

The key variable is **rep duration**. It takes 60 to 90 seconds of hard running
before your oxygen uptake actually climbs to its maximum. A 45-second rep
finishes before you ever get there — it's a fine workout, but it's training
something else. Reps of three to five minutes mean you spend two to four minutes
per rep at or near your true ceiling, and that accumulated time is the stimulus.

So: a set of 400m reps is intervals. A set of 3-minute reps at 3k–5k effort is
VO2 max work. Both are useful; only one of them targets your aerobic ceiling
directly.

## Why it works

Working at your maximum oxygen uptake pressures every link in the oxygen chain
at once — how much blood your heart moves per beat, how much plasma you carry,
how densely capillaries feed the working muscles, and how much of the delivered
oxygen those muscles actually extract.

It's a genuinely uncomfortable stimulus, and that discomfort is doing real work.
It's also the reason these sessions can't be run year-round: the training cost
is high and the returns flatten after six to eight weeks of consistent exposure.

## How to pace it

- **Effort.** 8–9 out of 10, climbing toward 9 by the end of each rep.
- **Heart rate.** 95–100% of maximum in the closing minute of each rep. Note
  that heart rate lags — it will still be climbing as the rep ends.
- **Race-pace shorthand.** Roughly 3k to 5k pace. Closer to 5k pace for
  five-minute reps, closer to 3k pace for three-minute ones.
- **Work-to-rest.** Close to 1:1. Three minutes hard, three minutes jogging.

The pacing error to avoid: these are not mile-pace reps. If you run the first
one at a pace you could only hold for 90 seconds, you'll fade badly and spend
the rest of the session below the intensity you came for.

## A sample session

**15 minutes easy warm-up, 5 × 3 minutes hard with 3 minutes of easy jogging
between, 10 minutes easy cool-down.**

That's 15 minutes of work — a substantial session. Starting out, run three reps
rather than five and build from there. As with any interval set, the reps should
be even; the fifth should look like the first.

## Where it fits in your week

Once a week, in a block of six to eight weeks, typically in the middle of a
training cycle rather than year-round. Outside that block, threshold work and
easy volume do more for less.

Keep the surrounding days easy. This is the hardest session in this guide and it
demands genuine recovery — most runners need two easy days after one.

## Common mistakes

- **Running them at mile effort and fading.** The classic error. You should
  finish the last rep at the same pace as the first, feeling like one more would
  have been possible but unpleasant.
- **Cutting the recovery too short.** Insufficient recovery means the next rep
  starts compromised, and you end up doing a long threshold session by accident.
- **Running them year-round.** The returns flatten and the fatigue doesn't. Use
  them as a block, then go back to threshold work.

## Track it in Kamee

Track it with GPS and use the splits afterwards to check the shape of the set —
even is good, descending means you opened too fast. If your route has an
out-and-back leg, compare reps run in each direction before blaming yourself for
a slow one; wind accounts for more variation in a 3-minute rep than most people
expect.
