## What it is

A race-pace run puts a substantial block of running at your goal race pace
inside a longer, otherwise-easy run. Not the whole run — a chunk of it, in the
middle or at the end.

It's a dress rehearsal. You're practicing the exact pace you intend to hold on
race day, on legs that already have some miles in them, ideally in the kit and
shoes you plan to wear, taking the fuel you plan to take.

## Why it works

Training adapts you specifically to what you practice. A runner who has spent
months at easy pace and 5k pace has never actually rehearsed the thing they're
about to do for three hours.

The physical case is straightforward: running at goal marathon pace recruits a
particular blend of muscle fibers and burns a particular mix of fuel, and doing
it repeatedly makes it more efficient. But the larger benefit is calibration.
Goal race pace should eventually feel *recognizable* — you should be able to
drop into it without looking at your watch, and know within a few seconds
whether you're on it.

That matters because the single most common way to ruin a race is to run the
first few miles too fast. A pace you've rehearsed a dozen times is a pace you
can hold from the gun without checking.

And it's a systems test. Race-pace runs are where you find out that a gel
disagrees with you, that a shirt chafes at 90 minutes, or that your goal pace is
optimistic — all of which are much better discovered now.

## How to pace it

Effort depends entirely on the distance you're targeting:

- **Marathon pace.** 5–6 out of 10. Comfortable early, and it should feel almost
  too easy for the first 20 minutes.
- **Half-marathon pace.** 6–7 out of 10.
- **10k pace.** 7–8 out of 10.

Get your goal pace from evidence, not hope — a recent race or a
[time trial](/blog/time-trial), adjusted for the distance you're targeting. A
goal pace pulled out of the air produces a session that teaches you the wrong
feel.

## Two sample sessions

**Marathon.** 90 minutes total, with 40 minutes at goal marathon pace in the
middle. Easy for 25 minutes, 40 at pace, easy to finish.

**10k.** 10 minutes easy warm-up, then 4 × 6 minutes at goal 10k pace with 2
minutes of easy jogging between, then 10 minutes easy.

Note that neither session is entirely at race pace. A run done wholly at goal
pace for the full race distance is a race, and it will cost you a fortnight.

## Where it fits in your week

Once a week in the specific phase of training — roughly the final six to eight
weeks before a goal race, when general fitness gives way to race-specific work.

It counts as your quality session for the week. Marathon-pace blocks often live
inside the long run, which is efficient but makes that run genuinely hard, so
plan the recovery accordingly.

## Common mistakes

- **Guessing a goal pace your fitness doesn't support.** Then you rehearse a
  pace you can't hold and learn nothing useful. Derive it from a recent time
  trial or race.
- **Running the whole session at race pace.** That's a race. Do a portion, not
  the lot.
- **Testing new shoes or gels on race day instead of here.** This session exists
  partly so that nothing on race day is new. Use it that way.

## Track it in Kamee

This is the session where live pace matters most — you're deliberately trying to
hold one specific number, and the feedback loop is the training. Track it with
GPS, watch pace through the race-pace block, and check afterwards whether your
splits held steady. Drifting slower through the block is the clearest possible
signal that your goal pace needs revising.
