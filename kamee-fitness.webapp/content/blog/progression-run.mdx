## What it is

A progression run starts easy and finishes fast. One run, one continuous effort
curve, no stopping — you simply run the last part quicker than the first.

The classic structure is thirds: an hour split into 20 minutes easy, 20 minutes
moderate, and 20 minutes at threshold effort. You can build it any way you like
as long as the direction of travel is one way.

It's the most efficient session in this guide, in the sense that it gives you a
substantial chunk of quality work inside what is otherwise an ordinary run, at a
recovery cost well below a full workout.

## Why it works

Two reasons, one physical and one behavioral.

Physically, a progression run puts your quality work on legs that are already
somewhat fatigued. Twenty minutes of threshold effort at the end of an hour is
harder — and more specific to how a race actually feels — than the same twenty
minutes taken fresh. You practice producing pace when the tank isn't full,
which is the only situation that matters late in a race.

Behaviorally, it trains restraint. Almost every runner's instinct is to go out
too fast and hang on, and almost every good race is run the other way around.
A progression run makes the correct pattern the only way to complete the
session. Do enough of them and the pacing discipline stops being a conscious
effort.

## How to pace it

Think in efforts, not paces, and let each third sit clearly above the last:

- **First third.** 3 out of 10. Genuinely easy — this is the part people get
  wrong.
- **Second third.** 5 out of 10. Steady and comfortable, base-run territory.
- **Final third.** 7 out of 10. Comfortably hard, around
  [tempo](/blog/tempo-run) effort. You can speak a short phrase, not a
  sentence.

The finish is not a sprint. If the last five minutes turn into an all-out
effort, you've converted a repeatable session into a hard one.

## A sample session

**45 minutes as 15 / 15 / 15.** Fifteen minutes easy, fifteen steady, fifteen
at comfortably-hard effort, then two or three minutes of walking to finish.

A gentler version, worth starting with: run 45 minutes easy and lift only the
final ten minutes. That's still a progression run, and it's a far more forgiving
introduction than committing to full thirds on day one.

## Where it fits in your week

Once a week at most, and it counts as your quality session — don't run a
progression run and a separate interval workout in the same three days.

It works especially well as a substitute for a formal workout during a busy
week, since it needs no track, no measured route, and no recovery intervals to
manage. It also makes a good long-run finisher: the last 15 minutes of a 90
minute long run, lifted slightly, is a progression run in miniature.

Keep the day after it easy.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting the first third too fast.** If you open at base pace, there's
  nothing left to progress into, and you end up running a moderately-hard hour
  with no clear stimulus.
- **Turning the last third into an all-out effort.** The final segment should
  be controlled and repeatable. Racing it converts the session into something
  that costs you two days.
- **Running one the day before a hard session.** It's cheaper than a full
  workout, but it isn't free — treat it as quality and schedule around it.

## Track it in Kamee

This is the session where live pace earns its keep. Track it with GPS and
glance at your pace at each third to confirm you're actually stepping up rather
than drifting. Afterwards, the splits tell you the whole story in one look: you
want three clear plateaus, each faster than the last, with no spike at the very
end.
