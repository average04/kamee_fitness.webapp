## What it is

Hill repeats are hard efforts run uphill, with a jog or walk back down as
recovery, repeated for a set number of reps. The reps run anywhere from 30
seconds to three minutes, on a grade of roughly 5 to 8 percent.

It's the closest thing distance running has to strength training that still
counts as running. Gravity supplies the resistance, so you get a genuinely hard
effort without needing a track, a stopwatch, or a measured route — just a hill
and a willingness to run up it several times.

## Why it works

Running uphill increases the force each stride has to produce. Your glutes,
hamstrings, and calves work considerably harder than they would on the flat at
the same effort, which builds power and improves running economy — how much
oxygen you burn to hold a given pace.

The clever part is what it doesn't cost you. Uphill running naturally shortens
your stride and reduces the vertical drop of each landing, so impact forces are
lower than in flat speedwork at comparable effort. You get an intense
cardiovascular and muscular stimulus with less pounding, which makes hills a
notably lower-risk way to introduce hard running.

There's a form benefit too. It's very difficult to overstride going uphill —
the ground arrives too early. Hill work tends to nudge runners toward a quicker
cadence and a foot that lands closer to underneath them, and that carries over
onto the flat.

## How to pace it

- **Effort.** 7–8 out of 10, roughly 5k to 10k effort. Hard, sustained,
  controlled.
- **By feel.** You should reach the top breathing hard but able to turn around
  and jog down without stopping.

Ignore pace entirely. Uphill pace numbers are meaningless as a target, and
chasing them is how a good hill session becomes a bad one.

## Form on the hill

- Short strides, quick cadence. Let the hill shorten your stride rather than
  reaching for more ground.
- Drive the arms. On a steep grade, the arms do real work.
- Lean from the ankles, not the waist. Folding at the waist compresses your
  breathing and shifts load off the glutes — the exact muscles you came here to
  use.
- Eyes up the hill, not at your feet. It keeps your chest open.

## A sample session

**15 minutes easy warm-up, 8 × 45 seconds uphill at hard effort, jogging back
down as recovery, 10 minutes easy cool-down.**

Find a hill with a steady 5–8% grade that takes at least a minute to climb, so
you're not turning around at the top. The jog down is your recovery — take it
genuinely easy, and add 15–30 seconds of standing at the bottom if you need it.

Starting out, run four or five reps. Add one per week.

## Where it fits in your week

Once a week as your quality session, particularly in a base or early build
phase where they substitute nicely for track work.

They're also an excellent bridge into faster running. If you're coming back from
a layoff or adding intensity for the first time, hills are a safer first hard
session than [intervals](/blog/interval-run) — same intensity, less impact.

Keep the day after easy.

## Common mistakes

- **Over-striding to reach further up the hill.** It feels productive and it
  isn't. Shorten the stride and raise the cadence instead.
- **Bending at the waist.** The most common form error under fatigue. It
  compresses your breathing and takes your glutes out of the movement.
- **Racing the descent.** This is where the injuries and the next-day soreness
  actually come from. Downhill running is eccentric loading — the muscles
  lengthen under tension, which causes far more damage than the climb.

## Track it in Kamee

Track the session with GPS so you get the elevation profile afterwards — that's
the part worth reviewing, since it confirms you actually ran the same stretch of
hill each time. Ignore the pace splits here; they'll look alarming and mean
nothing. Auto-pause will handle any standing around at the bottom.
