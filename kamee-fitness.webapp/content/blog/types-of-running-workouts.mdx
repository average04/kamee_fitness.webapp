## Why runners don't just run

Every run in this guide is the same activity with two dials set differently:
**how hard** and **how long**. That's it. A recovery run and a set of VO2 max
intervals are not different sports — they're different settings on the same two
dials, and each setting produces a different adaptation.

That's why training plans have variety in them. Easy running builds the aerobic
machinery but won't make you faster on its own. Hard running makes you faster
but can't be sustained in volume. You need both, in the right proportion, and
the proportion is the thing most runners get wrong.

The pattern that works, and that most serious distance runners converge on, is
**polarized**: a large majority of your running genuinely easy, a small minority
genuinely hard, and comparatively little in the moderate middle. Something like
four fifths easy and one fifth hard is the usual shape.

The middle is the trap. Moderately-hard running feels productive — you're
working, you're sweating, the pace looks respectable — but it's too hard to
accumulate volume and too easy to drive real adaptation. Runners who never plan
their intensity end up living there by default, training hard enough to be tired
and easy enough to not improve. Almost every plan below is designed, one way or
another, to keep you out of it.

## The four groups

**Foundation.** The aerobic base. Most of your weekly running lives here — easy
runs, base runs, the long run, and the recovery runs that let you keep doing
them. These build capillary density, mitochondria, stroke volume, and the
tendons and bones that everything else depends on. Unglamorous, and the reason
anyone gets faster.

**Speed.** Faster than comfortable, on purpose. One or two of these a week, no
more. Tempo runs raise the pace you can sustain; intervals and VO2 max reps
raise your aerobic ceiling; fartlek and strides add speed without the formality.
These are where the sharpening happens, and where overdoing it does the most
damage.

**Strength.** Terrain does the work — power and stability without a track. Hill
repeats give you an intense effort with less impact than flat speedwork, and
trail running builds stabilizing strength and ankle resilience that no road
route asks for.

**Race.** Sharpening, rehearsing, and testing what you've built. Race-pace runs
rehearse the day; a time trial tells you honestly where your fitness is; the
shakeout gets you to the start line loose.

## How to build a week out of these

A five-day week that works for most runners with some experience behind them:

- **Monday** — rest or recovery run
- **Tuesday** — workout (tempo, or intervals)
- **Wednesday** — easy run, with strides at the end
- **Thursday** — base run
- **Friday** — rest
- **Saturday** — easy run, with strides at the end
- **Sunday** — long run

Two rules matter more than the specific layout.

**Hard days are followed by easy or rest days.** Adaptation happens during
recovery, not during the session. Two hard days back to back gives you the
fatigue of both and the benefit of roughly one.

**One workout a week is enough for most people; two is the ceiling.** If you're
running three hard sessions a week, you are almost certainly running all three
of them below the intensity that would make them worthwhile.

If you're new, ignore the workout entirely. Spend six to eight weeks on
Foundation runs only — easy runs, base runs, and a long run — and let the
aerobic base and the connective tissue catch up before you add a Speed session.
Nothing in the Speed group is dangerous, but all of it is wasted on a base that
isn't there yet, and injury risk climbs sharply when speed arrives before
durability.

## Where to start

**If you're new to running:** [the easy run](/blog/easy-run) and
[the long run](/blog/long-run). Between them they'll take you further than any
workout will in your first year, and getting the easy run genuinely easy is the
single highest-value habit in the sport.

**If you run consistently but never do workouts:**
[the tempo run](/blog/tempo-run). It's the highest-return session for the
effort, and it's more forgiving of pacing errors than intervals are. If a formal
workout feels intimidating, [fartlek](/blog/fartlek-run) gets you most of the
way there with none of the structure.

**If you're training for a specific race:** add
[race-pace runs](/blog/race-pace-run) in the last six to eight weeks, and run a
[time trial](/blog/time-trial) first so the pace you rehearse is one your
current fitness actually supports.

## Track it in Kamee

Start a GPS track session for any of these and you'll get live pace while you
run and splits to review afterwards — which is most of what you need to tell
whether you actually ran the session you intended. Auto-pause handles the
standing around between hard reps. And the split history is where the honest
answer lives: if your easy runs and your tempo runs are converging on the same
pace, you've drifted into the moderate middle, and it's worth spreading them
back apart.
