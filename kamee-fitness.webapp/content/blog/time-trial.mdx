## What it is

A time trial is a solo, all-out effort over a fixed distance — most commonly one
mile or 5k — run to find out how fit you currently are rather than to compete
with anyone.

It's a measurement, not a race. No bib, no crowd, no tactics. Just you, a
repeatable route, and an honest effort from start to finish.

## Why it works

Almost every training pace you use should be derived from your current fitness,
and most runners derive theirs from a race they ran eight months ago or from a
number they'd like to be true.

A time trial replaces that guesswork with data. From a recent 5k result you can
set a threshold pace for [tempo runs](/blog/tempo-run), a rep pace for
[intervals](/blog/interval-run), and a sanity check on your easy pace. Every
other session in your week gets more accurate as a result.

It also tells you whether the last training block actually worked. Consistent
running feels roughly the same week to week even as fitness changes underneath
it — the effort stays constant while the pace quietly improves, which is
exactly why you can't feel your own progress. A time trial makes the change
visible.

And there's a secondary benefit: it's practice at the specific discomfort of
racing. Going deep on your own, with nobody to chase, is a skill.

## How to pace it

- **Effort.** 9–10 out of 10. This is genuinely all-out — you should have
  nothing left in the final minute.
- **Distribution.** Even, or very slightly negative. The first quarter should
  feel almost too controlled.

That last point does most of the work. A time trial where you go out hard and
fade badly measures your pacing judgment, not your fitness, and the number it
produces is useless for setting training paces.

## Getting a comparable number

The point is to compare results over time, so control what you can:

- **Same route, every time.** Flat, measured, and repeatable. A track is ideal;
  a flat out-and-back is fine.
- **Similar conditions.** Heat and wind both cost real seconds. A 5k in 30°C
  heat isn't comparable with one run in the cool.
- **Similar state.** Rested, not the day after a hard session, and at roughly
  the same time of day.

## A sample session

**15 minutes easy warm-up including 4 strides, 5k all-out, 10 minutes easy
cool-down.**

Warm up properly — a cold start costs more time than any pacing strategy will
save. The [strides](/blog/strides) at the end of the warm-up wake the legs up so
the opening minute doesn't feel like a shock.

For a shorter option, a one-mile time trial is less taxing, recovers faster, and
still gives you a usable benchmark.

## Where it fits in your week

Every six to eight weeks — roughly once per training block, and no more often.

Treat it as your hard session for that week, keep the days on either side easy,
and don't run one within a few days of a race you care about.

## Common mistakes

- **Running one every couple of weeks.** At that frequency you're racing, not
  testing, and the accumulated fatigue costs more than the data is worth.
- **Going out at a pace you can't hold.** Produces a number that reflects your
  pacing, not your fitness, and it's demoralizing on top of that.
- **Running it the day after a hard session.** You'll measure your fatigue.
  Time-trial into a rested state or don't bother.

## Track it in Kamee

Track it with GPS on the same route each time so the comparison is genuinely
like for like. Afterwards, the splits tell you whether you paced it well — flat
splits mean the number is trustworthy, a big positive split means you measured
your judgment rather than your engine. Keep the session in your history and the
next time trial has something honest to compare against.
