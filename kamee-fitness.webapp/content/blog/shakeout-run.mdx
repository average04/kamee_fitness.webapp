## What it is

A shakeout run is 15 to 25 minutes of very easy running, usually the day before
a race or on the morning of one, sometimes with a few
[strides](/blog/strides) at the end.

It is the shortest and easiest run in this guide, and the only one whose primary
job is not physical. It builds nothing. It's meant not to.

## Why it works

Two things happen in the days before a race, and a shakeout addresses both.

The first is stiffness. Race weekends involve sitting — in cars, on planes, in
hotel rooms — and a taper means you've been running less than usual. Both leave
legs feeling flat and heavy. Twenty minutes of gentle running restores normal
range of motion and blood flow, and legs that felt like concrete at breakfast
usually feel like legs again afterwards.

The second is nerves. The day before a race is mostly waiting, and waiting gives
you time to invent problems. A short run gives the day a shape, burns off some
restlessness, and — because you've done this exact thing a thousand times — is
reassuringly familiar.

There's a small priming effect too. Light activity the day before doesn't reduce
your freshness, and a few strides remind your legs what quick turnover feels like
so the first mile of the race isn't a cold shock.

It's worth being clear about what a shakeout can and can't do, because runners
often expect too much of it. It will not add fitness — that ship sailed weeks
ago, and nothing you do in the final 48 hours will make you faster. It will not
undo a bad taper or a missed block of training. What it does is remove a small
amount of friction: stiff legs, a restless afternoon, and the peculiar
sensation of not having run in two days. Those are minor problems, but they're
the only ones still available to solve, and solving them costs nothing.

This is also why the shakeout is optional. Plenty of good runners skip it
entirely and race well. If a day of complete rest leaves you feeling sharper
than a jog would, that's a perfectly reasonable read of your own body — the
run is a tool, not an obligation.

## How to pace it

- **The talk test.** Completely conversational, throughout.
- **Effort.** 2–3 out of 10. If you're wondering whether it's too easy, it's
  about right.
- **Heart rate.** Whatever it wants to be. Don't look.

Race-week legs often feel strange — sometimes sluggish, sometimes suspiciously
springy. Neither means anything about your race. Don't read the tea leaves.

## A sample session

**20 minutes very easy, plus 3 × 20 second strides.**

Run somewhere flat and pleasant. Finish feeling like you could comfortably do it
all again. If you're racing in an unfamiliar place, jogging the first half mile
of the course or the finish area is a good use of the time.

On race morning, a 10-minute version works well for shorter races, where it
doubles as your warm-up.

## Where it fits in your week

The day before a goal race, or the morning of one — sometimes both. It's also
useful after a long travel day, and it makes a reasonable first run back in the
days after a race, when the aim is to move rather than to train.

Outside of race weeks, what you're describing is a
[recovery run](/blog/recovery-run).

## Common mistakes

- **Stretching it out because you feel good.** Feeling good the day before a
  race is the taper working. Spending it on a 45-minute run is spending the
  thing you spent three weeks accumulating.
- **Adding surges to test the legs.** The legs will be fine. Testing them costs
  you a little sharpness and tells you nothing, because race-week legs feel
  strange regardless.
- **Skipping it entirely after a long travel day.** This is precisely when it
  does the most good — a day of sitting is the exact problem a shakeout solves.

## Track it in Kamee

Track it if you like the record, but this is the one run where the numbers are
beside the point — nothing in a shakeout is worth analyzing afterwards. If
you're racing somewhere new, the GPS route from your shakeout is genuinely handy
for finding your way back to the start in the morning.
