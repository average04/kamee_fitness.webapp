## What it is

A base run is your ordinary run — the one you'd do if nobody gave you a plan.
Forty to sixty minutes at whatever pace feels natural and steady, on a route you
know, without a target split or a workout structure to execute.

It's the least glamorous entry in any training plan and usually the most common
after easy runs. Most weekly mileage is made of base runs, and most of the
fitness that shows up on race day was built by them rather than by the sessions
you'd actually brag about.

## How it differs from an easy run

These two get used interchangeably, and the difference is real but small.

An **easy run** is deliberately held back. You are actively choosing to run
slower than your body wants to, because the point is to accumulate time on your
feet at minimum cost. A **base run** is simply your natural steady pace — the
gear you fall into when you stop thinking about it. It's a notch above easy, and
it's still comfortably aerobic.

The practical test: if you finish an easy run feeling like you barely ran, that's
correct. If you finish a base run feeling pleasantly worked but nowhere near
tired, that's also correct. If you finish either one needing to sit down, you ran
a workout by accident.

New runners are usually better served by treating everything as an
[easy run](/blog/easy-run) until the aerobic base is there, then letting base
runs emerge naturally.

## Why it works

The adaptations are the same ones easy running produces — more capillaries
feeding the working muscles, more mitochondria inside them, a stronger heart
stroke — just with slightly more stimulus per mile, and slightly more cost.

Base runs matter because they're where volume comes from. Training load is
roughly intensity multiplied by time, and the aerobic system responds mostly to
accumulated hours. You can't accumulate hours at threshold effort; you can
accumulate a great many at base pace.

## How to pace it

- **The talk test.** You can hold a conversation, but you'd rather not give a
  speech. Sentences are fine; paragraphs are work.
- **Effort.** Around 4–5 out of 10.
- **Heart rate.** Roughly 65–75% of maximum.

Don't chase a number. The pace that qualifies as "base" will move around by a
minute per mile across a year depending on fitness, heat, and how you slept, and
all of those versions are correct.

## A sample session

**45 minutes steady on rolling terrain.** Run out for a little over twenty
minutes, turn around, come back. No warm-up ritual required beyond starting
gently for the first five minutes, and no finishing effort.

Rolling terrain is a mild bonus rather than a requirement — the small
undulations vary the load on your legs without turning the run into a workout.

## Where it fits in your week

Base runs fill the space between your hard sessions and your long run. In a
five-day week you might see two base runs, one easy run, one workout, and one
long run. In a three-day week, one base run, one workout, one long run.

They also make an excellent replacement for a session you can't face. Swapping a
tempo run for a base run on a bad day costs you very little and keeps the week
intact.

## Common mistakes

- **Letting every base run creep toward tempo.** This is the slow drift that
  ruins training weeks. If your base runs keep getting faster, your workouts
  will get slower, and you'll end up doing everything at one moderate,
  unproductive intensity.
- **Adding volume and intensity in the same week.** Pick one. Increase weekly
  mileage or sharpen the sessions, not both.
- **Using it to make up a missed workout.** A missed session is gone. Turning
  Thursday's base run into Tuesday's intervals just moves the fatigue, and
  usually into your long run.

## Track it in Kamee

Track the run with GPS and let the splits do the auditing. If your base runs are
steadily creeping faster from week to week without feeling harder, that's
fitness. If they're creeping faster *and* the effort is climbing too, you're
drifting — and the split history is where you'll see it first.
