## What it is

Strides are short, controlled accelerations — 15 to 30 seconds each — where you
build smoothly up to near-full speed, hold it briefly, and ease back down. You
take a full minute or more of walking or jogging between them, and you do
somewhere between four and eight.

The most important thing to understand about strides is that they are **not a
workout**. Total hard running in a set of six strides is under two minutes, and
you should finish feeling fresher and springier than when you started, not
tired. If a set of strides leaves you fatigued, they were run wrong.

## Why it works

Most distance running happens in a narrow band of slow, repetitive movement.
Over months of that, your stride quietly shortens, your cadence settles, and the
fast-twitch fibres you rarely recruit get harder to recruit. Strides push back
against all three.

Running near top speed for a few seconds forces a longer stride, a quicker turn
over, and a fuller range of motion at the hip. It recruits muscle fibres that
easy mileage never touches and rehearses the neural patterns of running fast —
which is largely a coordination skill, not a fitness one.

The cost is almost nothing. That combination — meaningful stimulus, negligible
fatigue — is why strides can be added to easy days without disrupting anything.

## How to pace it

Think of each stride in three parts:

- **Build.** Accelerate smoothly over the first 5–8 seconds. Never explode from
  a standing start.
- **Hold.** 8–15 seconds at roughly your mile-to-800m effort. Fast, relaxed,
  tall posture, shoulders loose, jaw slack. Not a sprint — around 90–95% is the
  target.
- **Ease off.** Decelerate gradually rather than slamming to a stop.

Recovery is 60 to 90 seconds of walking or slow jogging between each one, and
you should feel genuinely ready before starting the next.

## A sample session

**After an easy run: 6 × 20 seconds on flat ground, with 90 seconds of walking
back between each.**

Find a stretch of flat grass, track, or quiet path. Do them at the end of an
easy run when you're warm, or before a workout as part of the warm-up.

Grass is a good surface for strides — it's forgiving, and the slight instability
adds a little foot and ankle work for free.

## Where it fits in your week

Two or three times a week, tacked onto the end of easy or base runs. They also
belong in the warm-up before any hard session or race, where they wake the legs
up and make the first fast rep feel far less abrupt.

Strides are one of the highest-value-per-minute things a distance runner can
add. If you've been running easy for months and wonder why fast running feels
foreign, this is usually the missing piece.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating them as sprints from a standing start.** Explosive starts are how
  hamstrings get pulled. Build into the speed — the acceleration should be
  gradual enough that a bystander couldn't name the moment you got fast.
- **Skipping the recovery.** Ninety seconds feels like a long time standing
  around. Take it anyway: the quality of each stride is the entire point, and
  incomplete recovery lowers it.
- **Doing them when already sore.** Strides on tired, stiff legs add injury risk
  and no benefit. Save them for a day when you feel decent.

## Track it in Kamee

Strides are short enough that GPS won't tell you much about any individual one —
20 seconds is at the edge of what satellite positioning resolves cleanly. Track
the run they're attached to, and judge the strides by feel instead. What you're
looking for is that the sixth felt as smooth as the first.
